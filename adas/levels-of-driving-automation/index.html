The six levels of driving automation

Level 2, Level 3, 'hands-off', 'eyes-off': what the SAE levels really say, and what they do not.

One scale for everyone

The levels come from SAE J3016, a recommended practice first issued in 2014 and revised in April 2021 together with ISO. It defines six levels, from Level 0 (no driving automation) to Level 5 (full driving automation), and applies to a feature that is engaged, not to a car as a whole: the same car can offer features of different levels.

The scale describes who does what: who steers and controls speed, who watches the road, and who takes over when something goes wrong. It is a vocabulary, not a safety rating.

Levels 0 to 2: the driver drives

Level 0: no sustained automation. Warnings and momentary interventions, such as automatic emergency braking, do not drive the car and stay at Level 0.

Level 1: the system controls either steering or speed, for instance adaptive cruise control.

Level 2: the system controls steering and speed together, but the driver must supervise all the time. 'Hands-off' systems such as Ford BlueCruise are still Level 2: the hands may leave the wheel on approved motorways, the eyes may not leave the road.

Level 3: the car drives, the driver stays available

At Level 3, within its operating conditions, the system performs the whole driving task and the driver may look away, but must take over when the system asks. In Germany, Mercedes-Benz DRIVE PILOT is approved up to 95 km/h on motorways in the S-Class and EQS, and BMW Personal Pilot L3 up to 60 km/h in the 7 Series.

Levels 4 and 5: no driver needed

At Level 4, the system does not need a person to take over within its operating conditions (a defined area, road type or speed). Level 5 would drive anywhere a person could, in any conditions.

There is no 'Level 2+' or 'Level 2.5' in J3016: those words are marketing, and the standard asks that the levels be used as whole numbers.
